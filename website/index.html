The earthquake app built for Kurdistan

Fast, trustworthy, scientifically correct earthquake information, in your own language. Bumelerze (bûmelerze, Kurdish for “earthquake”) is built by seismic-risk researchers who work on this region every day.

Live feed

What it does

One app, four jobs, built for how earthquakes actually happen here

  • 01

    Live earthquake feed

    Kurdistan first. Recent and historical earthquakes near you, combined from three independent scientific sources (USGS, EMSC, GEOFON), big, clear, and readable the moment you open the app, even on a weak connection.

How the science works

From three networks to a map you can trust

You don't need a seismology degree to follow this. Here is the chain from “the ground moved” to “here is what it did,” in three steps.

1. Three networks, one feed

Every earthquake is checked against USGS, EMSC, and GEOFON, three independent international monitoring networks. Bumelerze merges their reports of the same event into a single internal record, so you see one clear entry instead of three conflicting ones.

2. What you felt matters

Your “I felt it!” report is scored on the EMS-98 macroseismic intensity scale, the same kind of scale used in official damage assessments. Reports from the same area are aggregated into a community-observed intensity, real testimony, not a guess from a model alone.

3. Bumelerze SHAKEmaps

For earthquakes in the region, Bumelerze computes its own shaking-intensity map from ground-motion models and, where available, those same felt reports. Every map states its inputs and whether a scientist has reviewed it, no black box.

Open, cited data

We cite our sources. You can check our work.

Who it's for

Built for the people who feel it first

Anyone in the Kurdistan Region or Iraq who wants fast, trustworthy earthquake information in their own language, the moment it matters, not translated and not an afterthought. Emergency planners and response teams who need a real, real-time picture of how an earthquake was actually felt on the ground, from the people who felt it. Researchers and students who need open, citable seismic data for a region that has long been under-instrumented and under-studied.
